About

Seven years making cells do things they weren't supposed to. Now I've taken it into industry, where the problems move faster and the work pays for a life.

My PhD, obtained in the labs of Politecnico di Milano and Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, was in physics, but the lab was cross-disciplinary from day one — physicists working with biologists, engineers working with clinicians, everyone translating for everyone else. I became good at that translation. And at something harder to name: the kind of problem-solving that looks like luck from the outside. Machines that wouldn't work for anyone else would work for me. None of it was magic. Patience, and a refusal to accept that something broken couldn't be understood.

The work I'm most proud of isn't in any paper. It's a microscopy analysis macro with instance segmentation that reduced processing time by 80%. A custom double patch-clamp setup assembled from parts and stubbornness. Arduino code that ran overnight experiments so I didn't have to. Things that made the lab function better and made other people's work easier. I cared about that, probably more than I needed to.

I was still uncertain when I moved to the University of California. The uncertainty didn't last long. The hours saw to that, and so did a slower realization: the system works exactly as it's meant to. It just wasn't built for what I wanted from it.

What the US gave me, unexpectedly, was scale. A different kind of distance from everything familiar forces a certain clarity. I understood what I was good at, what I wanted, and what I was no longer willing to trade away. At 34, still not being able to afford a used car is just information.

The months between have been busy. Mornings: job applications and case studies. Afternoons: building. Python apps shipped solo, all integrating LLMs into real workflows. Not a portfolio flex. The problems are genuinely interesting, and the fastest way to understand a technology is to use it for something you actually care about.

The competitive intelligence tool maps a technical field in five minutes. The fragrance recommender applies NLP to olfactory note pyramids. The gym tracker normalizes training load by bodyweight so the numbers mean something over time. The knowledge map turns what I read into a graph I can explore. Each one started from curiosity. They're all on GitHub.

The transition has a name now: Application Specialist — Life Sciences Microscopy, at Zeiss. The commercial side of the instruments I spent years pushing past their limits. The job keeps me in labs, talking with scientists and clinicians, learning something new every time. It's also where I sharpen what I'm actually after: how to size up a technology fast, and how to solve problems that don't come with a manual. New territory, and I'm the one walking in.

Enough about the work.

Before I was thirty I'd visited most of the major art museums in Europe. I wrote my PhD thesis in LaTeX with a custom typography package because the default looked bad and that bothered me. Fragrance note pyramids get the same attention I once brought to experimental design. I care about fabrics, cities, the way a room is lit.

The same filter runs through everything else. Fiction, essays, poetry, manga, but only when the writing is good. Films for the craft or the ideas, rarely the spectacle. Video games that are atmospheric and story-rich, small precise indie things rather than MMOs. Metal for its technical density, singer-songwriters for the weight of the words. Trains over planes whenever I can: the scenery, the slowness, the feeling of actually crossing a distance.

Then there's the body. Football and volleyball for the team, the competition, the plain fun of it. Kickboxing and, lately, ballo liscio for the opposite lesson: both put me in the center and ask me to lead, to be decisive, to hold my ground, whether the thing across from me is an opponent or a dance partner. All of it is mindfulness by another name, the body fully present. The gym is the exception, less meditation than measurement, though I keep at the dips and pull-ups partly because calisthenics just looks cool.

It's the same sensibility behind the scientific work. Precision. Attention. The conviction that how something is made matters, not just whether it functions. I bring that to everything I do, which is either a strength or an inconvenience depending on the context.

"My life is too large to be shrinking again."
